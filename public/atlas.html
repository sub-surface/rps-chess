02 — openings

Every legal opening is drawn

JANKEN deals armies with 180° rotational symmetry, so Red's pieces always sit on the antipodes of Blue's. On a 3×3 board that permits exactly 192 starting positions — and under perfect play every single one of them is a draw, in all seven movement archetypes. There is no first-move advantage in any of them. That is a fact about the openings and not about the game: section 12 counts the positions where having the move decides everything, and the ones where it is a liability.

All 192 legal starts, each drawn. Hover to preview on the board, click to load.

The opening grid — and why its wins are a mirage

Line Blue's column up against Red's and you get 36 pairings, which do not all come out drawn. That looks like it contradicts everything above, and it is worth seeing why it does not.

A 180° turn reverses a column, so a layout can only ever produce a pairing where Red's order is Blue's read backwards — six of the thirty-six. Those six are lit below, and every one of them is a draw. The other thirty face armies at each other that no deal can produce; they are legal positions, reachable by editing the board, but they are not openings. Every win and every loss in this grid is one of them.

06 — symmetry

Twenty-four positions, one answer

A square board maps onto itself eight ways, and because every piece here shares one movement archetype, advancing rock→paper→scissors on both sides at once is a symmetry too. That is a group of 24. Apply any of them to the board and the verdict is obliged not to move — the strip below is the current position's whole orbit.

Symmetry could have stored this table 24× smaller. It is not used that way: a solved variant is 406 KB flat, which compresses to less than a photograph, and a flat table answers in one arithmetic step instead of a search. Symmetry earns its keep here as something to look at rather than as a compression trick.

09 — reachable play

Most of this table is unreachable

The tablebase covers every position the six pieces can form. A game does not: it starts from one of the … legal deals and only ever moves forward. Walking every legal continuation from every one of them gives the positions play can actually enter — … of the whole table.

Reachability, like every verdict here, ignores the clock: a position counts as reachable if some legal sequence arrives at it, not if a game that has not already drawn arrives at it. That makes this an upper bound on what play visits.

10 — the shape of material

Which pieces beat which armies

Chess counts material; JANKEN cannot. Rock against rock is not an even game, it is a dead one — neither side can ever capture, whatever either does. The grid pairs every combination of surviving types Blue can hold against every one Red can hold, and colours each cell by how Blue fares across all positions with that material and Blue to move. Click any cell to drop one of its positions onto the board.

11 — where the value is

What a square is worth

Take every position in which a given piece stands on a given square, with that side to move, and count how they come out. The result is a value map of the board — exact, not an evaluation function's guess at one. On nine squares with three pieces it is the whole of positional knowledge, printed.

12 — the value of the move

What it is worth to be the one who moves

Every placement sits in the table twice, once with each side to move, and the two entries need not agree. Comparing them measures the move itself. Where the entries differ in the mover's favour, having the move is an asset; where they differ against it, the mover is in zugzwang and would rather pass — which JANKEN does not allow. Chess treats zugzwang as an endgame curiosity. On nine squares it can simply be counted, for every archetype at once, and the count says something about the pieces.

13 — beyond the pocket board

5×5, 9×9, and what solving them would cost

The 3×3 is not the game people play. 5×5 and 9×9 are, and neither will ever be solved — but the size of what we are not solving is exact arithmetic, not a guess. Counting distinguishable arrangements of each side's material gives every bar below: three pieces a side on the 3×3, six on every board above it. Only the first bar is a table that exists.

15 — the blocking law

Two thirds of every contact is a wall

This is the difference from chess, stated as arithmetic. A piece meeting an enemy can take it only if it beats it, which is one type in three; the other two are pieces it cannot touch and cannot pass. Mobility therefore falls as the board fills, and it falls faster than crowding alone explains.

16 — what search is worth

How much thinking this game needs

The bot that plays you on the front page is the same program at every level; only its budget changes. That makes it a measuring instrument: give it a fixed number of positions to look at, ask it for a move, and mark the move. On this board the marking is exact, because the table knows the answer. On every larger board the marker is the same search given a far bigger budget — a better opinion, not the truth, and labelled as such.

17 — method

How this was built, and what it does not claim

Six labelled pieces, each captured or on one of nine squares, give 207,775 placements — 415,550 counting whose turn it is. The solver walks every legal move with engine.js, then works backwards from the endings: a position is won when some move reaches a lost one, lost when every move reaches a won one, and drawn when it never resolves at all.

Two honest caveats

These are positional values, in the sense chess tablebases mean it: they ignore the clock. JANKEN ends a game that has gone 60 actions without a capture and awards it to whoever leads on material, which can turn a theoretical shuffle into a win. For the king variant that rule changes 5,952 of 415,550 verdicts — 1.43% — and it does not change any legal opening. Threefold repetition, the other history rule, cannot change a positional verdict at all: a forced win never needs to revisit a position.

The second caveat is narrower. Every variant here gives all three pieces the same archetype. Mixed assignments — King's field, say — are solvable the same way, but they lose the rock→paper→scissors symmetry, so the pictures in section 06 would be a third the size.

Regenerate with npm run tablebase. The generator audits everything it writes: every value must equal the best reply available, every distance must match the best line, and every verdict must survive all 24 symmetries.
